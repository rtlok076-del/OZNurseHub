The Orchard’s Best Year

A small story about a record harvest, a golden ribbon, and why one extreme year is usually followed by an ordinary one.

Watch · 1 minuteRead · 2 minutesLearning & reflection

The full story

Who gets the blame when a record year turns ordinary?

In the orchard beyond the crossroads, old trees grew apples every year. Most years were ordinary. But one summer, a single tree called Old Rowan grew more apples than anyone had ever seen. Its branches bent almost to the ground.

Senna the bilby was delighted. “Old Rowan is the best tree in the land!” she declared, and she tied a golden ribbon around its trunk.

The next year, Old Rowan grew a perfectly normal number of apples. Not bad, just ordinary.

The mocking fox sneered. “See? The golden ribbon made it lazy. Praise always spoils things.”

Meanwhile, at the other end of the orchard, a scrawny tree had grown only three apples the year before. Senna had shouted at it and pruned it hard. This year, it grew an ordinary crop too.

“Aha!” said the fox. “Shouting works and praise doesn’t!”

Bayes the sparrow fluttered between the two trees. “Did the ribbon or the shouting really change anything?” she asked. “Or was last year just unusually lucky for one tree and unusually unlucky for the other?”

Senna thought back. Last year, Old Rowan had caught the morning sun after a branch fell from a neighbour, and the rain had been just right. The scrawny tree had been nibbled by caterpillars. Those were rare strokes of luck, good and bad.

“Extreme years are often followed by ordinary ones,” said Bayes. “Not because of ribbons or shouting, but because luck doesn’t usually strike twice in a row.”

Senna untied the golden ribbon and smiled. She would judge her trees by many seasons, not by one.

After a very good or very bad result, expect things to drift back towards normal, and don’t give the credit or blame to whatever you did in between.

On the ward too: one odd reading? Recheck it, watch the trend, and escalate if you’re worried.

Regression to the mean describes how an unusually high or low measurement is often followed by one closer to the average, simply because chance played a part in the first result. It doesn’t mean things are pulled back by a force; it means extreme results usually contain some luck that doesn’t repeat. This matters in everyday life and in healthcare. That is why good decisions rely on repeated measurements, and why research uses comparison groups to tell real effects from natural drift.

A moment to reflect

After one unusually good or bad result, what else would you look at before giving credit or blame?

When one reading looks odd, what helps you tell a real change from a one-off — and when would you escalate?
