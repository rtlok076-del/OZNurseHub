Fable Story

Mina and the Two Seeds

A small story about starting with what you already know, letting each new clue tip the scale, and never judging evidence in a vacuum.

Watch · 1 minuteRead · 1 minuteLearning & reflection

The full story

Start with what you know. Let the evidence tip the scale.

In a cedar grove, a raven named Mina found two seeds beneath the same tree. One seed had fallen from a peach, the other from a plum, but from the ground they looked nearly identical.

Mina asked the squirrel archivist how to decide which seed would grow into a sweeter fruit.

The squirrel replied, “Begin with what you believed before you looked: peach trees are rarer here than plum trees. That is your prior odds.”

Then he weighed the clues. The first seed was slightly fuzzier, and its shell had a faint ridge. “Each clue tilts the scale,” he said. “Their combined tilt is the Bayes factor.”

Mina multiplied the grove’s prior odds by the tilt of the evidence and found her answer: the peach seed, though the rarer kind, was more likely than she had first guessed.

She tried to skip the prior and use the evidence alone, but the squirrel stopped her. “Evidence is never interpreted in a vacuum. Posterior belief is prior belief updated by likelihood.”

Mina planted the peach seed in the sunniest patch and the plum seed by the stream, smiling at how a good inference turns rumour into a reasoned bet.

Posterior odds equal prior odds times the Bayes factor. Bayes’ rule is not just a formula for probabilities; it is the algebra of rational belief revision under uncertainty.

A moment to reflect

What did you already know before the newest clue arrived, and how much weight should it still carry?

Which clue would tip your scale the most, and which might you be giving too much weight?
