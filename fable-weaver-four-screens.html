The Weaver’s Four Screens

A small story about why harm slips through when the holes in several safeguards line up, and why fixing the system matters more than blaming one tired helper.

Watch · 1 minuteRead · 2 minutesLearning & reflection

The full story

Harm slips through when the holes line up.

At the edge of the marsh stood the village infirmary, where a gentle badger healer mixed medicines for every creature who came limping in. One stormy night, a wise old weaver noticed a jar of sleeping syrup sitting on the shelf meant for cough syrup.

“How did that get there?” asked Bayes the sparrow, fluttering onto the bench.

The weaver hung four woven screens in a row across the doorway. Each one had a few holes. “Every good habit is a screen,” she said. “The label on the jar. The healer reading it twice. The helper checking again. The patient asking, ‘Is this my usual medicine?’”

Then she blew a puff of dandelion seeds at the screens. Most seeds caught on the first screen. A few slipped through its holes and stuck on the second. Only when the holes in all four screens lined up did a single seed float right through to the other side.

“Tonight,” said the weaver, “the labels looked alike, the healer was tired, the helper was busy, and the patient was too sleepy to ask. Four small holes, all in a row.”

The mocking fox sniffed from the window. “Just blame the tired badger and be done with it.”

“Blaming one screen won’t stop the next seed,” said the weaver. So the villagers moved the sleeping syrup to a different shelf, gave it a bright red label, and added a quiet corner for double-checks. The holes stopped lining up, and the seeds stayed caught.

That’s the Swiss cheese model. Every safeguard has holes. Harm slips through when the holes line up.

Mistakes rarely come from one bad moment. Fix the holes in the system, not just the creature who fell through them.

The Swiss cheese model is a way of thinking about safety used in healthcare, aviation and other high-risk work. Each safeguard, such as clear labels, double-checks, staff training and patients speaking up, is like a slice of Swiss cheese: helpful, but never perfect, because every slice has holes. Harm usually happens only when the holes in several slices line up at the same moment. Rather than simply blaming the last person involved, safety teams look for ways to add extra layers and shrink the holes, so that one slip is caught before it reaches anyone.

A moment to reflect

Where might several small holes in your own checks line up, and what would shrink one of them?

When something goes wrong, do you stop at the last person involved, or look at the system that let the holes line up?
