Palliative care · Study guide

The phases of palliative care

A palliative care phase describes what a person and their family need right now. It is not a countdown and it does not have to move in order.

Australian services commonly use the phase definitions from the Palliative Care Outcomes Collaboration (PCOC). Knowing them helps you follow handover, understand care plans and know when to speak up.

Before you start

  • Phases describe needs, not diagnosis. They are based on a holistic assessment of the person and their family or carers, including symptoms, function and how the family is coping.
  • Phases can change in any direction. A person can move from deteriorating back to stable, or from stable straight to unstable.
  • PCOC reports four phases (stable, unstable, deteriorating and terminal). Bereavement support after death is still an important part of palliative care, so it is included here as a fifth stage of care.
  • The phase is decided by the team. As a student, your observations and conversations are valuable input, so share them.
Phase 1

Stable

Symptoms and problems are adequately controlled by the current plan. The family or carers’ situation is relatively steady.

Guide to response

Monitor, and continue or fine-tune the current care plan.

Nursing focus points

  • Regular, structured symptom assessment: pain, breathlessness, nausea, fatigue, appetite, sleep and mood.
  • Notice small changes in function, such as mobility, eating and personal care.
  • Support conversations about goals of care, preferences and advance care planning.
  • Educate and support carers, and check how they are managing.
  • Document a clear baseline so changes are easy to spot.

Ask yourself: what does a good day look like for this person, and what matters most to them?

Phase 2

Unstable

A new problem that was not expected, or a rapid increase in the severity of an existing problem, needs an urgent change to the care plan. A sudden change in the family or carers’ circumstances that affects care can also make the phase unstable.

Guide to response

Intervene, refer and escalate urgently. The plan is changed and reviewed within 24 hours.

Nursing focus points

  • Recognise the change early and escalate promptly using ISBAR.
  • Check the documented goals of care and resuscitation plan so escalation matches what the person wants.
  • Reassess frequently and after every intervention, and record the response.
  • Use comfort measures: positioning, a calm environment, mouth care and reassurance.
  • Keep the family informed and supported. Sudden changes are frightening for them too.

Ask yourself: is this new or worse than expected, and who needs to know now?

Phase 3

Deteriorating

The care plan is meeting anticipated needs but needs regular review, because the person’s overall function is gradually declining, existing problems are slowly worsening, or new but expected problems appear. Gradually increasing family or carer distress also fits here.

Guide to response

Refer and escalate as needed, and review interventions as planned.

Nursing focus points

  • Anticipate needs: pressure injury prevention, mouth care, continence, falls risk and equipment.
  • Review eating and drinking with the person and team as needs change.
  • Revisit goals of care as function declines, and make sure the plan still fits.
  • Watch for carer fatigue, and involve social work, allied health or specialist palliative care.
  • Make sure the team has planned how symptoms will be managed if things change.

Ask yourself: what is likely to happen next, and is the plan ready for it?

Phase 4

Terminal

Death is likely within days.

Guide to response

Provide care for the dying person and their family.

Nursing focus points

  • Recognise common signs of dying: more time asleep, less interest in food and fluids, changes in breathing pattern, noisy breathing, and changes in skin colour and temperature.
  • Focus on comfort: mouth and eye care, gentle repositioning, pressure care, continence care and a peaceful environment.
  • Follow the team’s plan for reviewing routine observations and interventions that no longer help comfort.
  • Assess for distress and escalate uncontrolled symptoms promptly.
  • Explain what the family may see, invite them to be involved, and respect cultural and spiritual needs.

Ask yourself: is this person comfortable, and does the family know what to expect?

After death

Bereavement and post-death support

The person has died. Care continues for the family, carers and those important to them.

Guide to response

Offer bereavement support and information to the family.

Nursing focus points

  • Provide respectful care after death, following local guidance and the person’s cultural and religious wishes.
  • Give the family time and privacy, and explain the practical next steps simply.
  • Offer information about bereavement support services.
  • Watch for family members who may need extra support.
  • Look after yourself: debrief with your supervisor or educator. It is normal to be affected.

Ask yourself: who is supporting the family, and who is supporting me?
